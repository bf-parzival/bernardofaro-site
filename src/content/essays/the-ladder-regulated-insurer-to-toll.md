Everyone wants to be the toll. Almost nobody wants to climb to it.

I argued once that the agent economy will be owned from one of two positions: infrastructure ownership — the rails everything routes through — or domain monopoly — the deepest model of one specific thing. I framed them as two different games. I've since decided that was the wrong framing. They're not two games. They're two ends of one ladder, and the whole strategy is which end you start at.

## The rungs

You start at the end you can actually reach, and you climb toward the end that's actually worth owning.

- **Rung 0 — Broker.** Sell the cover. Win the relationships. Run the machine on your own book first, so it's a product you've used before it's a product you sell.
- **Rung 1 — System of record.** The machine that ran your book gets sold to carriers as their operations. Now you're not a broker, you're the canonical record of what happened — upstream of everyone.
- **Rung 2 — MGA.** The record turned into loss data. Loss data earns the pen — the right to decide what gets written and at what price.
- **Rung 3 — Platform.** The layer you built for yourself, opened to others. They build regulated products on your substrate; you take an override.
- **Rung 4 — Toll.** Capacity competes to deploy its balance sheet through you. You take a thin rate on the flow and carry none of it.

Two things move the whole way up: **scope widens** (one country, then a continent) and **take rises** (a commission, then a pen, then a network rate). That's why the value grows faster than linearly between rungs.

## Each rung pays for itself

This is the part that makes the climb survivable. Every rung is a real company on its own. The broker is a business before the MGA exists. The MGA is a business before the toll is a line in a deck. You don't burn five years of runway betting on the top — you earn at each step, and each step manufactures the exact data the next one needs.

## Why insurer-first beats infra-first

Here's the asymmetry that picks the starting end. You can't underwrite what you can't price. You can't price without loss data. You can't get loss data without holding risk. You can't hold risk without a licence and a book.

The pure-infrastructure player skips all of that — and then can't cross back. They can build a beautiful rail and still have nothing to bind, because binding needs the domain and the licence they routed around. The regulated insurer can walk *up* the ladder toward infrastructure. The infrastructure player can't walk *down* into regulated risk. One direction is open; the other is a wall.

So you start as the thing that's hard to become — licensed, inside the domain, holding real risk — and you climb toward the thing that's hard to defend once you're there.

---

Climbing is slower than leaping, and I won't pretend otherwise. Pure software compounds fast; insurance drags capital, regulation and trust behind it. That's exactly why you climb in rungs instead of promising the summit — you de-risk each step with proof before you take the next, and you let the lower rungs, where you actually win, fund the contest for the top.

The toll is the prize. The ladder is the only way up that doesn't get you killed on the way.
