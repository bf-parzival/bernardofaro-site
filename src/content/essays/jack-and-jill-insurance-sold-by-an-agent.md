The question isn't whether agents will buy insurance. They will — it's a boring, recurring, rules-based purchase, exactly the kind a competent agent should make without you. The question is who they call when they do.

Today insurance is sold by humans to humans through a funnel that everyone hates: forms, brokers, back-and-forth, weeks. Now picture the funnel with the humans removed. A buyer's agent assembles the risk — who, what, where, how exposed — and calls an endpoint that quotes and binds. That's not a far-future scenario. The pieces are shipping.

## Jack and Jill

Two roles. **Jack** is the buyer's agent: it gathers and structures the risk on behalf of a company or a person. **Jill** is the other side: it matches that structured risk to carrier appetite and binds the cover. Jack does discovery; Jill does placement. The whole human funnel collapses into one machine-to-machine handshake.

This needs no grand agent economy to arrive. It rides the distribution market that exists right now. Herald already indexes hundreds of carrier quote-and-bind APIs. Sure and ACORD have shipped insurance over the Model Context Protocol — the wire format agents already speak. The rails for Jill exist. What's missing is someone licensed standing at the end of them.

## The economics flip

Here's why this matters beyond convenience. When a machine does the buying, the cost of selling changes shape.

`CAC` stops being ad spend and becomes *presence* — being the endpoint the buyer's agent already trusts to call. And `cost-to-serve` trends toward zero, because no human touches the policy. That second number is the one that's quietly enormous: it means the small policies that were never worth selling — the per-shift, per-trip, per-project covers — finally pay. A whole long tail of risk that conventional distribution can't afford to write becomes writable.

<div class="callout">
<span class="callout-label">Worked example</span>

A founder's operations agent is spinning up the company in a second market. It needs D&O and cyber that follow into the new geography. Today that's a broker, a week, three email threads.

With Jill it's a call: the agent passes the structured risk, gets a quote that matches a carrier's real appetite, and binds — in the time it took to read this sentence. The founder never opened a form. The broker margin didn't disappear; it moved to whoever Jill is.

## The moat is the licence

There's a reason this favours a licensed operator over a clever model. Binding insurance is regulated — IDD and DMIF in Europe — and that doesn't evaporate because the buyer is a machine. Someone has to be the licensed entity that legally places and binds the cover. A raw model can structure the risk all day; it cannot be Jill. That regulatory floor is exactly the kind of constraint that looks like friction and works like a moat.

And it composes with everything else. Jack & Jill is one of three ways the same engine distributes: hand-sold today, embedded in the platforms that already see the activity, and self-serve through agents tomorrow. One licence, one binding API, three channels.
